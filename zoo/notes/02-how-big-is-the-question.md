[The last note](../whose-journey/) ended with three people at a ticket counter and one more observation about
them. They were not only different parties. The traveller was dealing with the next ninety
seconds, the employee with a procedure that repeats every day, and the operator with a
decision that will stand for years.

My first instinct was to turn that into a grid.

## Three bands

The split is old. Robert Anthony divided the decisions inside an organisation into strategic
planning, management control and operational control: setting the objectives, making sure
resources are used to reach them, and making sure a specific task actually gets done.[ref:anthony-1965]
Most people now say strategic, tactical and operational, and everyone knows roughly what is
meant.

It carries over to a single person without much effort. Where to live, whether to own a car,
which job to take: decided rarely, and they stand for years. Whether to buy a monthly pass,
which route becomes the habit: weeks or months. Which train, which ticket, which door: now.
Transport research has argued for a while that the long decisions shape the short ones, and
that daily travel only makes sense once you know where in a life it happens.[ref:lanzendorf-2003]

So the plan looked simple. Every actor gets the same three bands, every item the extraction
model finds gets put in one of them, and the map has a vertical axis as well as a horizontal
one. You would see at a glance that the complaints all sit at the bottom.

## Where the bands stop working

The easy sentences are easy. "The machine would not take my card" is operational for anyone
who could have written it.

The hard ones are more interesting. Take a sentence like "I bought the monthly pass so I would
stop arguing with myself about taking the car." Is buying the pass tactical? Here it is the
means, and the end is an argument the writer keeps having with themselves, which is about a
car, which is about how they want to live. Now take the same purchase by someone whose
employer pays for it. It is an administrative step with no why behind it at all. Same act,
different height.

The operator's side behaves the same way. A fare zone boundary is strategic to whoever drew
it. To the employee at the counter it is a rule, as fixed as the floor. To a planner revising
the zones next year it is this month's work.

What sentences like these contain, reliably, is not a band. It is a direction. "So I would",
"because", "so that we can": the text says that one thing serves another. It almost never
says how far either of them is from the top.

## Height is a relation

Consumer research has worked this way for decades. A means-end chain links a concrete
attribute of a product to the consequence it brings, and that consequence to a value the
person holds.[ref:gutman-1982] Laddering is the interview technique that climbs the chain:
you ask why that matters, then why that matters, until the answers stop changing.[ref:reynolds-1988]
Nobody in that tradition fixes the levels in advance. The top is wherever the ladder
happened to stop.

Taken seriously, that changes what the map stores. The unit becomes an edge between two
items, with an "in order to" on it and the sentence it was read from. Height is something
you read off a chain once you have one. A sentence that does not say why stays where it is,
unclimbed, instead of being promoted to a level I guessed for it.

That is also the version I can defend. A band would be my interpretation. An edge has a quote
behind it.

The three words stay useful for talking about a chain. They just do not go into the data.

## Felt here, caused there

Once the edges point upward, the counter scene looks different.

[diagram:felt-and-caused]

The traveller's chain climbs from the ticket to getting to work and, perhaps, to managing
without a car. The operator's chain climbs from staffing the counter to setting the fare
zones to paying for the network. The pain sits at the bottom of the first chain: which
ticket, now, with a queue behind. Its cause sits in the middle of the second, in a zone
structure decided at a desk, possibly years ago, by people who will never stand in that
queue.

That edge does not go up or down. It goes across, from one actor to another and from one
height to another, and a journey map has no place to draw it. My suspicion is that a good
part of the pain worth fixing in mobility looks like this, felt at the bottom of one chain
and caused somewhere in the middle of another. I cannot show that yet.

## What this means for the texts

This is the part that worries me.

The texts I work with are complaints, forum threads, reviews and interviews. People write
those about the bottom of their own chain. Nobody posts about the zone structure. They post
about the ninety seconds at the counter. The decisions further up, and especially the ones on
somebody else's chain, are written down elsewhere, in tariff documents, planning reports and
board papers, if they are written down at all.

So a model trained on what people write will find the pain reliably and the cause rarely. It
will produce a precise map of the bottom rung. An opportunity tree built on that map gets
pinned to the place where the pain is felt, which is often the one place where nothing can be
changed.

## What I changed

The requirement for the next model is now an edge rather than a band: two items, the
"in order to" between them, and the sentence that says so. Edges within one actor's chain
come first, because the texts I have contain them. Edges that cross from one actor to
another come later, and probably not from the same texts.

There will be no band field. Three labels that sound right are easy to add to a format and
very hard to take out again once something downstream depends on them.

## What comes next

Up and down is only one direction. A chain says why someone does something. It does not say
what they did before it, or what had to happen first, and a journey is mostly that: one thing
after another. Order turns out to be harder to read out of text than height, for a reason
that has to do with how many texts it takes to see it at all.
