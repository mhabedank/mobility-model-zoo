# Research: architecture options for scout-large

Date: 2026-11-27. Status: research only, nothing trained yet. Baseline: scout-large 0.1.3 (weights of 0.1.0).

The notes in `notes/` hold the sources: [backbones](notes/backbones.md), [span heads](notes/span_heads.md), [compression and speed](notes/compression_speed.md) and [confidence](notes/confidence.md). Each fact in the notes is tagged as read in the primary source during this research or not. How the current model is built and measured is in the [recipe](../../recipes/scout-large.md).

In short: scout-large gains more from its head than from its backbone.

Yes, there is a better setup for scout-large. The biggest lever is not the ELECTRA architecture from a LinkedIn post by Knowledgator about EdgeCoder-31M, though. It sits in three other places. **Quality** comes mainly from a different task formulation: instead of token BIO over 512-token windows, the model tags sentence and clause units, relevance is derived from the items, and windows are aligned to sentence boundaries. This targets the measured error where a job that spans several sentences falls apart into two items (item-matching F1 0.59). **Size and speed** come most reliably from distilling today's large model into a student with 20M to 110M body parameters, plus ONNX INT8 and less window overlap. On paper, this brings the 8.1 s for the 9,240-character interview down to about 0.3 to 1.5 s; this has not been measured. **Reliable confidence** comes from calibration (temperature scaling, a dedicated span calibrator) and from soft labels built from the disagreement of the LLMs, not from a backbone change. No encoder from 2025/26 has been shown to beat XLM-R-large at German token tagging. mDeBERTa-v3-base ties it on German NER at 96.4 F1, with about a third of the body parameters (and, because of the more expensive attention, about half the compute). That makes it the strongest documented candidate. The EdgeCoder numbers in the LinkedIn post match Knowledgator's own preprint. They apply to English GLUE, though, and rest on a single seed. The 31M model is English only, and the multilingual 61M variant trails mmBERT-small on multilingual NER and QA. "ModernELECTRA" is also a DeBERTa backbone with RTD training, not a new architecture beyond BERT. EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M therefore belongs in the comparison as a candidate, but it is no basis for a migration. German and English are must-haves, and more languages will follow. That rules out English-only and German-only backbones and drops vocabulary trimming (see [Internationalisation is a hard requirement](#internationalisation-is-a-hard-requirement)).

## The bottleneck is the BIO head, not the encoder

The measurements show a clear pattern. The attributes are already good (kappa 0.76 to 0.85); the item boundaries and the relevance are not. The typical error, a multi-sentence job becoming two items, is a problem of segmentation consistency, not a weak grasp of the labels. A BIO tagger with an independent softmax per token is free to set a new B- at the start of every sentence. The argument-mining literature already traces imprecise span boundaries to exactly this local independence assumption of the BIO tagger ([Eger et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.06104)). A CRF forbids illegal transitions, but it hardly prevents an unnecessary B-. A larger or newer encoder does not change this mechanism.

The best fit is a model that works on exactly the unit that is annotated. Scout items are by definition whole sentences or clauses. This leads to a setup in four steps. First, the text is split deterministically into units, keeping the character offsets. Then each unit is pooled from the encoder. On top sit a small contextualiser (one or two transformer layers or a BiLSTM) and a unit-level CRF with B/I/O. The pattern is established: hierarchical sentence labelling with a sentence CRF was 2 to 3 points above the then state of the art on PubMed abstracts ([Jin & Szolovits 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.06161)), and argument segmentation has been solved as sentence-level BIO ([arXiv 2112.00160](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.00160)). In this setup, "the item continues in the next sentence" becomes an explicit I-versus-B decision that sees the context across the sentence boundary. The output stays verbatim, because an item is always a union of contiguous input units. An equivalent span formulation is a biaffine scorer over pairs of start and end units ([Yu et al., ACL 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.577)). With 15 to 30 units per window it costs practically nothing. The research found no direct comparison of sentence and token formulations with the same encoder on claim or argument spans. The effect on scout is therefore a hypothesis with a good mechanistic basis, not a published result.

Two more error sources sit in the same place. The first is stitching. An item that crosses a window boundary is only half visible in each of the two windows, and each window can set its own B-. Overlapping windows reduce such fragmentation ([arXiv 2609.29682](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29682)), but long cross-sentence dependencies remain the main victims of short windows ([arXiv 2305.17273](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.17273)). A cheap fix is to align windows to sentence boundaries, assign each unit to the window in which it is most central, and merge at unit level only after stitching. The second is relevance. A CLS head on a single 512-token window never sees the whole document. If the teacher defines relevance as "contains at least one job, pain or gain", an independent relevance head and the span head will contradict each other. A relevance score built from pooled unit or item scores across all windows removes this contradiction. The loss normalisation is also worth a check. With nominal weights of 0.5/0.5, the token loss sums over hundreds of tokens while the document loss is a single term. The actual weighting can therefore differ strongly from the nominal one. For the attributes, pool the merged item (mean plus first and last token plus a length embedding) and use an ordinal loss for the ordinal evidence type. The attributes are already good, so this has lower priority.

As a ready-made alternative to a custom build, GLiNER2.5 is an option. It offers span extraction, classification and span attributes in one encoder pass. `gliner2.5-multi-v1` (287M, mDeBERTa) uses a boundary architecture without a fixed span width, is Apache-2.0 and can be fine-tuned with LoRA ([GLiNER2 Wiki](https://github.com/fastino-ai/GLiNER2/wiki)). Its documented strength is zero-shot NER with short entities, though. On CrossNER, GLiNER2 reaches 0.590 F1 against 0.599 for GPT-4o, and hierarchical extraction, the counterpart of "item plus attributes", was not benchmarked ([arXiv 2507.18546](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18546v1)). The checkpoints with a span grid (GLiNER-v1, `gliner2-*-v1`) limit the span width and therefore do not fit items across several sentences. Even the authors of the latest study on multilingual universal NER note that in-domain fine-tuned models are clearly ahead of zero-shot universal models ([arXiv 2601.06347](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06347)). GLiNER2.5-multi is therefore a sensible baseline in the comparison, not an expected winner.

## No 2025/26 encoder beats XLM-R-large at German tagging

The new generation of encoders is clearly better at classification and QA, but not at token classification. mmBERT-base beats XLM-R-base on XNLI (77.1 against 74.6) and TyDiQA (74.5 against 70.5), but trails it by 3.2 points on WikiANN NER (58.2 against 61.4). The paper has no comparison with XLM-R-large ([mmBERT, arXiv 2509.06888](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06888)). The authors attribute the NER weakness to the Gemma-2 tokenizer lacking a consistent prefix whitespace during pre-training ([HF Blog mmBERT](https://huggingface.co/blog/mmbert)). For German, the best direct evidence is in the EuroBERT study (XGLUE NER, German): **XLM-R-560M 96.4, mDeBERTa-v3-280M 96.4, EuroBERT-610m 96.0, EuroBERT-210m 91.8, mGTE-305M 94.0** ([EuroBERT, arXiv 2503.05500](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05500)). The EuroBERT authors write themselves that their model trails XLM-R on NER. They explain this with tokenizer fertility: token tasks benefit from large vocabularies such as XLM-R's 250k tokens, compared with the 128k of Llama-3 (ibid.). The absolute values are unusually high for training described as English only, so the protocol may differ from classic CoNLL F1. The ranking of the models is still clear. English models such as ModernBERT are out: at 81.5 to 89.4 on German NER they do not fit a corpus that is 28% German (ibid.).

The compute column follows from a simple argument: the cost per token grows with the non-embedding parameters, because an embedding lookup is only a gather. In XLM-R-large, about 256M of the 560M parameters are in the embedding matrix. The specs come from the model cards and papers ([mmBERT-base](https://huggingface.co/jhu-clsp/mmBERT-base), [mDeBERTa-v3-base](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base), [EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M](https://huggingface.co/knowledgator/EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M), [LFM2.5-Encoder](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-Encoder-350M)), the WikiANN values for the small models from the mmBERT paper and the ModernELECTRA paper ([arXiv 2611.31495](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31495)). These values come from different papers with different language sets and are not directly comparable. Depending on the paper, mmBERT-small is once ahead of and once behind Multilingual-MiniLM.

This gives a ranking for scout. It already applies the internationalisation requirement of the next section:

1. **mDeBERTa-v3-base** is the best documented replacement: RTD pre-training, the same 250k tokenizer as XLM-R, a tie on German NER and about a third of the body parameters, but because of the more expensive DeBERTa attention about half the compute. This fits the general finding that RTD learns more efficiently per parameter. ELECTRA-large matched RoBERTa with about a quarter of the pre-training compute ([arXiv 2003.10555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10555)), and mDeBERTa-base was 3.6 points above XLM-R-base on XNLI ([DeBERTaV3, arXiv 2111.09543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09543)). In this sense the LinkedIn post points in the right direction, but the strongest RTD candidate for scout is a model from 2021, not EdgeCoder.
2. **mmBERT-base** is the bet on long context and has the widest language coverage. One finding is easy to miss: as a backbone for GLiNER-style span scoring, that is with pooled spans instead of BIO on the first subword, mmBERT was the best of five multilingual backbones (0.509 against 0.441 to 0.456 for XLM-R-base) ([arXiv 2601.06347](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06347)). A unit-level head as described in the first section avoids exactly the word-boundary weakness of the mmBERT tokenizer. The choice of head and the choice of backbone are therefore linked.
3. **mmBERT-small** as the base of a scout-small: 42M body parameters, about 7× less compute, same language coverage as mmBERT-base.
4. **EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M**, with reservations: the smallest serious multilingual candidate, but only 46 languages, weaker token-level results than mmBERT-small and a single seed (see [What is left of the LinkedIn post](#what-is-left-of-the-linkedin-post)).

**EuroBERT-610m** is more expensive than today, EuroBERT-210m is weak on German NER, and both cover only 15 languages. For **LFM2.5** there are no NER numbers, but there is a licence with a revenue threshold ([litert card](https://huggingface.co/litert-community/LFM2.5-Encoder-350M)). None of these models has fine-tuning results in the range of about 1,000 examples. All advantages of the new encoders were measured on large training sets. A comparison on scout data therefore needs at least three seeds and a learning-rate sweep per backbone. EuroBERT, for example, was tuned with ten log-spaced learning rates between 1e-5 and 1e-4 ([arXiv 2503.05500](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05500)). Differences below 1 to 2 F1 are noise at this data size.

## Internationalisation is a hard requirement

The model owner has set German and English as must-haves. More languages will be added later to widen the analysis. This changes several decisions in this report.

**Only genuinely multilingual backbones qualify.** XLM-R covers 100 languages, mDeBERTa-v3 about 100 (CC-100). mmBERT covers 110 languages in the main phase and 1,833 in the final decay phase ([notes/backbones.md](notes/backbones.md)). EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M covers 46: all 24 EU languages, 12 Slavic languages and Latin. It stays in only if the expansion stays in Europe, and the ModernELECTRA paper itself reports poor token-level results on unseen languages ([arXiv 2611.31495](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31495)). Out are EuroBERT (15 languages), ModernBERT, EdgeCoder-31M, NeoBERT and Ettin (English only) and ModernGBERT (German only). The notes do not record LFM2.5's language list; it is out for the licence and missing evidence given above.

**Vocabulary trimming is dropped.** Trimming the vocabulary to German and English would lock out every later language. It also saves RAM, not latency (see the next section).

**The unit-level head helps.** A head on sentence and clause units depends less on how each language is split into subwords, which helps cross-lingual transfer. In exchange, sentence segmentation must work in every target language. If CJK or another script without spaces between words is ever added, the segmenter has to handle it. The notes did not evaluate any language-specific clause segmenter ([notes/span_heads.md](notes/span_heads.md)).

**The distilled student must be multilingual.** The unlabeled distillation corpus should already contain the future languages, so the student does not have to be distilled again when they arrive.

**Every language needs its own test data.** `pilot-v2` covers only German and English. Every new language needs its own small test set with reference labels. Calibration thresholds, and any conformal sets, should be set per language. The 2026 work on conformal NER stratifies by language as well ([arXiv 2601.16999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16999); [notes/confidence.md](notes/confidence.md)).

**The language mix must be set on purpose.** `span-train-v1` missed its own target of at least 30% German and ended at 28% ([`span-train-v1.yaml`](../../../../configs/productdev/jtbd/span-train-v1.yaml)). Each new dataset version needs an explicit target per language, and the sources to meet it.

**The setup is cheap to extend.** Teacher labels from an LLM and the string-based quote repair work in any language. A new language costs licensed source texts and some euros of teacher labels, not human annotation.

## Distillation plus INT8 bring the 8.1 s under two seconds

Speed on CPU depends almost only on the number and width of the layers, the precision and the number of redundant tokens. The architecture tricks of the ModernBERT family (unpadding, FlashAttention-2, local-global attention) are at heart GPU features. The speedups of mmBERT (more than 2× at variable length, about 4× at long context) were measured on GPU ([arXiv 2509.06888](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06888)). For DeBERTa-style attention, and so also for EdgeCoder, fast kernels exist only as a GPU/Triton implementation (FlashDeBERTa). The ModernELECTRA paper contains **no CPU, ONNX or quantisation benchmarks** ([arXiv 2611.31495](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31495)), and the model card targets CUDA for long inputs ([HF Card](https://huggingface.co/knowledgator/EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M)). No independent CPU latency comparison under ONNX between mmBERT, EuroBERT, EdgeCoder and XLM-R exists. The times below are therefore extrapolations from parameter counts, not measurements.

The most reliable way to a small model is to distil the existing large model, not a new backbone that has to learn from 1,000 chunks. For multilingual NER, distilled students kept about 95 to 96% of the teacher F1 at 6× to 13× compression. XtremeDistil reached 88.64 F1 on WikiANN in 41 languages with 28M parameters, against 91.86 for the mBERT teacher with 179M ([arXiv 2106.04563](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.04563)). The teacher can also add soft labels to any number of unannotated mobility texts. NuNER showed that a 125M encoder trained on LLM-annotated data keeps up with GPT-4 in-context learning after fine-tuning, with 56 times fewer parameters ([NuNER, EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.660)). Unfiltered, noisy pseudo-labels can hurt a student, though ([SLaM, arXiv 2302.03806](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.03806)). Soft labels, a confidence filter and a fixed LLM-labelled validation set are therefore part of the recipe. A student with the same tokenizer as XLM-R (XLM-R-base, Multilingual-MiniLM, and with limits mDeBERTa and EdgeCoder-M, which use the equally large mDeBERTa tokenizer) can learn directly from token logits and hidden states. A student with a different tokenizer, such as mmBERT, needs labels projected to word or unit level. Whichever student is chosen, it must be multilingual, and the unannotated corpus should include the languages that are planned next.

After distillation come the deployment levers. Dynamic INT8 quantisation through ONNX Runtime typically gives a 2× to 3× speedup on CPU ([Optimum quantisation guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/optimum-onnx/onnxruntime/usage_guides/quantization); [luizedu91 INT8 NER](https://huggingface.co/luizedu91/ner-bert-lenerbr-onnx-int8)). The effect on NER ranges from negligible (0.954 to 0.948 F1 in a BioBERT thesis, [Chalmers](https://odr.chalmers.se/bitstreams/2a911d3d-e854-467b-9a02-6b9ad5d58216/download)) to catastrophic: Microsoft lost about 14 F1 on a production NER transformer and switched to FP16 ([Microsoft Open Source Blog](https://opensource.microsoft.com/blog/?p=90966)). INT8 is therefore a required experiment, but only with validation. If quality drops by more than about 0.5 F1, the head and the outer layers are excluded from quantisation or calibrated statically. Vocabulary trimming to about 60k German and English tokens would cut RAM and disk use clearly: XLM-R-large would have about 365M instead of 560M parameters, about 0.8 GB less in FP32. For XLM-R-base this caused no measurable loss on German NLI and sentiment ([Ushio et al. 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.981)). Latency barely changes, though, and NER was not tested in the study. Because of the internationalisation requirement, trimming is not recommended: it would lock out every language added later, for a gain in memory only. The cheapest gain of all is window overlap. A 9,240-character interview is roughly 2,300 to 2,700 tokens. With 512-token windows and 128 tokens of overlap, about 33% of the compute is duplicate work; with 32 to 64 tokens of overlap at sentence boundaries it is only 7 to 14%. Where interviews run in one batch, add thread pinning (four intra-op threads, no tokenizer parallelism) and sorting by length.

| Step | Size | Expected time for the interview (4 cores) | Quality risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Today: XLM-R-large, PyTorch FP32, overlap 128 | 560M, 2.5 GB RAM | 8.1 s (measured) | – |
| Overlap 32–64 at sentence boundaries + ONNX graph fusion | unchanged | ~5–6 s (estimate) | none or positive |
| + dynamic INT8 (per channel) | weights ~4× smaller | ~2–3 s (estimate) | −0.1 to −14 F1, validate |
| Student XLM-R-base or mDeBERTa-v3-base, FP32 | ~280M (~130M with trimming, not recommended) | ~2–3 s (estimate) | ~2–5% relative (estimate from XtremeDistil) |
| Student MiniLM / mmBERT-small / EdgeCoder-M, FP32 | 45–140M, body 21–42M | ~0.6–1.5 s (estimate) | ~3–6% relative (estimate) |
| Small student + INT8 | < 1 GB RAM | ~0.3–0.7 s (estimate) | as above plus INT8 risk |

Two limits apply. Long context does not automatically speed things up on CPU: a single 8k pass with global attention can be slower than 16 windows of 512 tokens. Measure lengths of 1k to 2k tokens first. And INT4 or weight-only quantisation mainly helps memory-bound LLM decoders. Encoders are compute-bound, and INT4 puts at risk exactly the precision at the span boundaries.

## Reliable confidence comes from calibration and soft labels, not from RTD

There is exactly one piece of evidence for RTD as a confidence argument: a large 2022 study recommended ELECTRA as the best-calibrated common encoder, alongside larger models, temperature scaling and focal loss ([Xiao et al., arXiv 2210.04714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04714)). A later paper found DeBERTa worse calibrated than BERT on several tasks ([arXiv 2412.15269](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15269)). There are no calibration data for mDeBERTa, XLM-R or German token classification, and no systematic study at all for the 2025/26 encoders. The effect of the backbone is small and inconsistent. Temperature scaling closes much of the gap in-domain at practically no cost ([Desai & Durrett, EMNLP 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07892)).

What matters more is where the miscalibration sits. Structured outputs such as NER tags and spans are clearly worse calibrated than simple classification ([Jagannatha & Yu, ACL 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04361)). Scout's item score, an aggregation of token probabilities, is therefore the most likely weak spot, not the relevance head. Jagannatha and Yu treat each span as a binary event "correct or not" and calibrate this event with a small calibrator. This beat existing methods on NER and QA and, used as uncertainty-aware decoding, even improved task performance (ibid.). For scout this means: a logistic regression over a few span features gives a calibrated P(item correct). The features are mean, minimum and length-normalised token probability, the probabilities at the boundary tokens, the length, the item kind and the relevance score. For the relevance head and the attribute heads, 105 validation rows support only one or two parameters: temperature or Platt scaling, one temperature per head, no isotonic regression. The 0.70 threshold is then set again on the calibrated probability. Expensive Bayesian methods are not worth it. In the only systematic study of uncertainty in transformer NER, no method beat the simple max-softmax baseline at token level. SNGP was clearly worse, and cheap single-pass methods such as Mahalanobis distance with spectral normalisation kept up with MC dropout and ensembles ([Vazhentsev et al., ACL 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.566.pdf)). MC dropout with K samples costs K encoder passes and is not an option on four CPU cores.

The biggest confidence gain is likely to come from the training signal. Scout learns from hard labels of an LLM teacher, and the two reference LLMs disagree on 1,574 items. Soft targets from the distribution of annotations measurably improve calibration; this is documented for human annotators ([Findings EMNLP 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/223247/1/2023.findings-emnlp.355.pdf)). In a 2026 study, a BERT classifier trained on the LLM's class plus verbalised confidence as a soft target was even better calibrated than the LLM itself ([arXiv 2605.11954](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11954)). Concretely, a document that the teacher and the reference LLMs rate as relevant 2:1 gets the target 0.67. A token that lies in the spans of two of the three LLMs gets P(I) ≈ 0.67. The research found no study that tests exactly this recipe for span extraction; it is an A/B candidate. It needs an honest evaluation: kappa 0.54 at n = 147 has a 95% interval of 0.25 to 0.76. The agreement of the LLMs with each other is the upper bound, and all metrics should be reported separately for consensus and disputed items. Formal guarantees through conformal prediction now exist for NER too ([arXiv 2601.16999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16999)). With 105 calibration documents, though, the realised coverage at a 90% target varies between about 85 and 96% (Beta(96, 10), standard deviation ≈ 0.028). Angelopoulos and Bates consider n ≈ 1,000 enough for most purposes ([arXiv 2107.07511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)). Until then, a review band with two thresholds (accept, review, reject), chosen from risk-coverage curves with document-grouped cross-validation, is the more honest tool. With more languages, these thresholds are set per language.

## What is left of the LinkedIn post

The core numbers are right; the conclusion for scout does not hold. All checked claims come from Knowledgator's own preprint ([ModernELECTRA, arXiv 2611.31495](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31495), 23 Nov 2026, not peer-reviewed) and the model card. Secondary coverage repeats them without checking ([Data Phoenix](https://dataphoenix.info/news/modernelectra-boundary-window-preprint)).

|---|---|
| EdgeCoder-31M: 31M parameters, 8 layers, ~1T tokens, 8,192 context | **Confirmed** by the preprint |
| GLUE 86.0, ahead of Ettin-32m (83.5), MiniLM-L12 (84.6), BERT-base (84.7) | **Confirmed as self-reported**: one seed, baselines taken from other papers; the authors themselves warn against over-reading tenths of a point |

The evidence is in the preprint ([arXiv 2611.31495](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31495)) and on the model card, which confirms German, Apache-2.0 and loading through `AutoModelForTokenClassification` ([HF Card](https://huggingface.co/knowledgator/EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M)). Some things still speak for EdgeCoder-M-61M. It uses the same 251k tokenizer as mDeBERTa, so its fertility is NER-friendly. With 28M body parameters it is the smallest serious multilingual candidate, and the licence is clean. Against it stand an age of four days at the time of research, a single seed, weaker token results than mmBERT-small, no measurement at all on CPU, and coverage limited to 46 mostly European languages. EdgeCoder-M is therefore a candidate for the distillation student, not a replacement for XLM-R-large. The sender is worth noting: Knowledgator also builds GLiNER-multitask and GLiClass ([arXiv 2406.12925](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12925v2); [arXiv 2508.07662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.07662v1)). EdgeCoder is clearly meant as a compact backbone for exactly such schema-based heads, and that is where it meets scout's actual problem.

## Six experiments in a fixed order

The order follows one logic: first be able to measure, then pull the cheap and safe levers, then change the formulation, and only then swap and shrink the backbone. A backbone comparison on the old BIO formulation would optimise the wrong bottleneck. A student distilled from today's model inherits its split errors.

Every experiment is evaluated per language, German and English separately, not only on the pooled benchmark. A change that helps English and hurts German does not pass.

| # | Experiment | Effort | Expected effect | Decision criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Measurement basis and data**: document-grouped 5-fold cross-validation, ≥ 3 seeds, bootstrap intervals over documents, split by consensus and disputed items, inter-LLM kappa as upper bound, all scores per language (DE, EN); label several thousand additional chunks with 2–3 LLMs, with a set target share per language | low (LLM cost) | Without this step, differences below ~0.2 kappa cannot be detected (today's CI 0.25–0.76); more data improves every later experiment | Confidence intervals narrow enough to separate 2 F1, per language |

Conformal prediction and risk control come only when several hundred calibration documents are available, per language. Until then, the review band from step 1 is enough.

## Conclusion

The LinkedIn post asks the right question and gives the wrong answer for scout. It is right that RTD-trained encoders get more out of pre-training per parameter, and that a 560M model is hard to justify for this task. But the fitting RTD candidate for German-English tagging is the unspectacular mDeBERTa-v3-base. A fresh 31M model for English, benchmarked only by its makers, is not. More important: the measurements suggest that the gap between 0.72 and 0.91 arises mainly because scout decides at token level what the teacher annotates at sentence level. No backbone closes this gap by itself.

This reverses the usual order. First the large model is made better, through a unit head, soft labels and more teacher data. Then it serves as the teacher for a small student on thousands of unannotated texts. Size, speed and confidence are then results of this pipeline, not properties bought with a model card. Because German and English are must-haves and more languages will follow, every step stays multilingual: the backbone, the student, the distillation corpus and the evaluation per language. Vocabulary trimming is not recommended for the same reason. The biggest open question is how well the small candidates do with only about 1,000 chunks on German sentence spans. Nothing has been published on this, and step 3 is the only way to find out.

## Open questions for the owner

- **Which languages come next?** European only, or also CJK, Arabic or others? This decides whether EdgeCoder-Multilingual-61M stays in the comparison (it covers 46 mostly European languages and does poorly on unseen ones) and how sentence segmentation must work (scripts without spaces between words need a different segmenter).
- **Should a scout-small be a separate model in the zoo?** The distilled student from step 4 (for example on mmBERT-small) could replace scout-large in place or be published next to it as its own model with its own card and release bar.
